## 2.1 Discharge Tube Experiment

### 2.1.1 Construction and Working of Discharge Tube (Discovery of Electron and Proton)
- **Construction:** A discharge tube is a long glass tube equipped with two metal electrodes (cathode and anode) connected to a high-voltage source. It is also connected to a vacuum pump to control the internal gas pressure.
- **Working Conditions:** Under normal pressure, gases are poor conductors. However, when the pressure is reduced to very low levels (e.g., $0.01 \text{ torr}$ or $0.001 \text{ mmHg}$) and a high voltage (5000–10000 V) is applied, the gas begins to conduct electricity.
- **Discovery of the Electron (J.J. Thomson, 1897):** Under low pressure, invisible rays were observed traveling from the negative electrode (cathode) towards the positive electrode (anode). These rays produced a greenish fluorescence on the glass wall. Because they originated from the cathode, they were called **Cathode Rays**. Thomson proved they were streams of negatively charged particles, later named electrons.
- **Discovery of the Proton (E. Goldstein, 1886):** Goldstein used a discharge tube with a **perforated cathode** (a cathode with holes). He observed luminous rays passing through these holes in a direction opposite to the cathode rays. Because they traveled through the canals of the cathode, they were called **Canal Rays** or **Positive Rays**. These rays were positively charged ions formed when cathode rays ionized the residual gas in the tube.

### 2.1.2 Properties of Cathode Rays and Positive Rays

#### a. Cathode Rays
1. They travel in **straight lines** perpendicular to the surface of the cathode.
2. They cast sharp shadows of opaque objects placed in their path, proving their straight-line propagation.
3. They can drive a small paddle wheel placed in their path, proving they possess **mass and kinetic energy** (they are material particles).
4. They are deflected towards the positive plate in an electric field and are deflected in a magnetic field, proving they are **negatively charged**.
5. They can produce X-rays when they strike a heavy metal target (anode).
6. ** High-Yield Fact:** The specific charge ($e/m$ ratio) of cathode rays is **constant** ($1.7588 \times 10^{11} \text{ C/kg}$) and is absolutely independent of the nature of the gas in the tube or the material of the electrodes. This proved the electron is a universal constituent of all matter.

#### b. Positive Rays
1. They travel in a straight line in a direction **opposite** to the cathode rays.
2. They are deflected by electric and magnetic fields, but in a direction opposite to that of cathode rays, proving they are **positively charged**.
3. ** High-Yield Fact:** The $e/m$ ratio of positive rays is **not constant**. It depends heavily on the nature of the gas used in the discharge tube because positive rays are the ionized gas molecules themselves. The maximum $e/m$ ratio is obtained when Hydrogen gas is used, leading to the discovery of the fundamental proton.

## 2.2 Planck's Quantum Theory

### 2.2.1 Energy, Frequency, Wavelength and Wave Number Relationship
Max Planck proposed that energy is not emitted or absorbed continuously, but rather discontinuously in the form of discrete, small packets called **quanta** (or **photons** in the case of light).

**Mathematical Relationships:**
The energy ($E$) of a single quantum is directly proportional to its frequency ($\nu$).
$$ E \propto \nu \implies E = h\nu $$
Where:
- $h$ is Planck's constant = $6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$

Since frequency ($\nu$) and wavelength ($\lambda$) are related by the speed of light ($c$):
$$ \nu = \frac{c}{\lambda} $$
Substituting this into Planck's equation gives the relationship between energy and wavelength:
$$ E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} $$
Furthermore, the wave number ($\bar{\nu}$) is defined as the reciprocal of the wavelength ($\bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda}$), representing the number of waves per unit length.
$$ E = hc\bar{\nu} $$

> **Summary for MCQs:** Energy is directly proportional to frequency and wave number, but inversely proportional to wavelength. High-energy radiation has a high frequency, high wave number, but short wavelength.

## 2.3 Bohr's Atomic Theory

### 2.3.1 Description of Bohr's Atomic Theory
Niels Bohr modified Rutherford's model by applying Planck's Quantum Theory to explain the stability of the atom and the hydrogen spectrum.
**Key Postulates:**
1. Electrons revolve around the nucleus in fixed, circular paths called **orbits** or **stationary states**.
2. While revolving in a particular orbit, an electron neither emits nor absorbs energy.
3. The angular momentum ($mvr$) of an electron is quantized; it must be an integral multiple of $\frac{h}{2\pi}$.
 $$ mvr = \frac{nh}{2\pi} \quad \text{(where } n = 1, 2, 3 \dots\text{)} $$
4. Energy is absorbed when an electron jumps from a lower to a higher orbit, and emitted (as a photon) when it falls from a higher to a lower orbit. The energy of the photon is:
 $$ \Delta E = E_2 - E_1 = h\nu $$

### 2.3.2 Calculating Radius and Energy of Revolving Electrons
*(Note: Be prepared to apply these formulas directly in the exam without deriving them).*
- **Radius of the $n$-th orbit ($r_n$):**
 $$ r_n = 0.529 \times \frac{n^2}{Z} \text{ Å} $$
 *(For Hydrogen, $Z=1$. Radius of the 1st orbit $r_1 = 0.529 \text{ Å}$). Radius is directly proportional to $n^2$.*
- **Energy of the electron in the $n$-th orbit ($E_n$):**
 $$ E_n = -13.6 \times \frac{Z^2}{n^2} \text{ eV} $$
 *(Or $-2.18 \times 10^{-18} \times \frac{Z^2}{n^2} \text{ J}$). Energy is inversely proportional to $n^2$. The negative sign indicates that the electron is bound to the nucleus.*

### 2.3.3 Spectral Lines of Hydrogen Atom
When excited electrons in hydrogen atoms fall back to lower energy levels, they emit photons of specific wavelengths, producing a line spectrum.
- **Lyman Series:** Falls to $n_1 = 1$. UV region.
- **Balmer Series:** Falls to $n_1 = 2$. Visible region. *(Historically the first to be discovered)*.
- **Paschen Series:** Falls to $n_1 = 3$. Infrared region.
- **Brackett Series:** Falls to $n_1 = 4$. Infrared region.
- **Pfund Series:** Falls to $n_1 = 5$. Far Infrared region.

### 2.3.4 Calculating Wave Numbers of Photons of Spectral Series
The **Rydberg Equation** is used to calculate the wave number ($\bar{\nu}$) of a spectral line:
$$ \bar{\nu} = R_H \left( \frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2} \right) $$
Where:
- $R_H$ is the Rydberg constant = $1.09678 \times 10^7 \text{ m}^{-1}$
- $n_1$ is the lower energy level.
- $n_2$ is the higher energy level.
To find wavelength, simply invert the result: $\lambda = \frac{1}{\bar{\nu}}$.

### 2.3.5 Defects of Bohr's Atomic Theory
1. **Multi-electron Atoms:** It successfully explains the spectrum of Hydrogen and single-electron species ($He^+$, $Li^{2+}$), but fails entirely for atoms with more than one electron.
2. **Zeeman Effect:** It cannot explain the splitting of spectral lines under the influence of a strong **magnetic field**.
3. **Stark Effect:** It cannot explain the splitting of spectral lines under the influence of a strong **electric field**.
4. **Fine Structure:** High-resolution spectrometers showed that single spectral lines actually consisted of multiple closely spaced lines, which Bohr could not explain.
5. **Violation of Uncertainty Principle:** Bohr assumed electrons move in fixed circular orbits with precisely known radii and velocities, directly contradicting Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle.

## 2.4 X-Rays and Atomic Numbers

### 2.4.1 Definition of 'X-rays'
X-rays are high-energy, penetrating electromagnetic radiations with very short wavelengths, typically ranging from **$0.01 \text{ nm}$ to $10 \text{ nm}$**.

### 2.4.2 Production and Uses of X-Rays
- **Production:** X-rays are produced when rapidly moving electrons (emitted from a hot filament cathode) are suddenly decelerated by striking a heavy metal target (the anode). The kinetic energy of the electrons is converted mostly into heat (>99%) and partly into X-rays (<1%).
- **Continuous vs. Characteristic:** Continuous X-rays are produced by general deceleration (Bremsstrahlung). Characteristic X-rays are produced when the incoming electron knocks out an inner-shell electron of the target atom, and an outer-shell electron drops down to fill the vacancy, emitting a specific X-ray photon.
- **Uses:** Medical radiography (bone fractures), treating cancer (radiotherapy), analyzing crystal structures (X-ray crystallography), and security scanners.

### 2.4.3 Moseley's Experiment
H.G. Moseley bombarded different metal targets with electrons and analyzed the characteristic X-rays emitted. He discovered a systematic mathematical relationship: the frequency of the X-rays emitted depended on the nature of the target metal.

### 2.4.4 Moseley's Law and its Significance
- **Moseley's Law:** The square root of the frequency ($\nu$) of the characteristic X-ray emitted is directly proportional to the atomic number ($Z$) of the target element.
 $$ \sqrt{\nu} = a(Z - b) $$
 *(Where $a$ and $b$ are constants for a specific X-ray series).*
- **Significance:** 
 1. It proved that **Atomic Number ($Z$)**, not atomic mass, is the fundamental property of an element.
 2. It resolved anomalies in Mendeleev's periodic table (which was based on atomic mass).
 3. It allowed Moseley to predict the existence of undiscovered elements (e.g., Technetium, Promethium) by finding gaps in the atomic number sequence.

## 2.5 Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle and Quantum Numbers

### 2.5.1 Orbital Concept based on Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle
Heisenberg stated that it is impossible to determine both the exact position and the exact momentum (velocity) of a microscopic particle (like an electron) simultaneously with absolute precision.
$$ \Delta x \cdot \Delta p \ge \frac{h}{4\pi} $$
Because of this uncertainty, we cannot say an electron follows a precise fixed "orbit". Instead, we must rely on quantum mechanics to define 3-dimensional regions of space where the *probability* of finding an electron is maximum. These regions are called **Orbitals**.

### 2.5.2 Compare Orbit and Orbital
| Feature | Orbit (Bohr Model) | Orbital (Quantum Mechanical Model) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Path** | Well-defined, 2D circular path around nucleus. | 3D region of maximum probability (>90%). |
| **Certainty** | Violates Heisenberg's Principle. | Strictly obeys Heisenberg's Principle. |
| **Capacity** | Can hold up to $2n^2$ electrons. | Can hold a maximum of 2 electrons. |
| **Shape** | Circular. | Different shapes ($s$ is spherical, $p$ is dumbbell). |

### 2.5.3 The Four Quantum Numbers
Quantum numbers are a set of four values that completely describe the position, energy, and orientation of an electron in an atom.
1. **Principal Quantum Number ($n$):** Determines the main energy shell (K, L, M, N...) and size of the electron cloud. Values: $1, 2, 3 \dots$
2. **Azimuthal / Secondary Quantum Number ($l$):** Determines the subshell ($s, p, d, f$) and the shape of the orbital. Values: $0$ to $(n-1)$.
 - $l=0 \implies s$ (spherical)
 - $l=1 \implies p$ (dumbbell)
 - $l=2 \implies d$ (double dumbbell)
 - $l=3 \implies f$ (complex)
3. **Magnetic Quantum Number ($m$):** Determines the spatial orientation of the orbital in an external magnetic field. Values: $-l$ to $+l$ (including 0).
 - *Example:* For $p$-subshell ($l=1$), $m$ = $-1, 0, +1$ (representing $p_x, p_y, p_z$).
4. **Spin Quantum Number ($s$):** Determines the direction of the electron's spin on its own axis. Values: $+1/2$ (upward spin) or $-1/2$ (downward spin).

### 2.5.4 Position and Distribution of Electrons Using Quantum Numbers
Using the four quantum numbers, you can pinpoint any electron. 
- * Question Example:* Find the quantum numbers for the valence electron of Sodium ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^1$).
- The outermost electron is in $3s^1$.
 - $n = 3$ (3rd shell)
 - $l = 0$ ($s$-subshell)
 - $m = 0$ (only one orientation for $s$)
 - $s = +1/2$ (first electron in the orbital)

## 2.6 Dual Nature of Electron

### 2.6.1 Dual Nature of Electron with Reference to de-Broglie Equation
Louis de-Broglie proposed that just as light exhibits both wave and particle properties, all material particles (like electrons) also possess a **dual nature**—acting as both a particle (having mass) and a wave.
He combined Einstein's mass-energy equation ($E=mc^2$) with Planck's equation ($E=h\nu$) to derive the **de-Broglie wavelength**:
$$ \lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv} $$
- Wavelength ($\lambda$) is inversely proportional to momentum ($p = mv$).
- This wave nature is only observable for microscopic particles like electrons. For macroscopic objects (like a cricket ball), the mass is so large that the wavelength becomes insignificantly small and unobservable.

## 2.7 Electronic Configuration

### 2.7.1 Rules of Electronic Configuration
1. **Aufbau Principle:** Electrons fill lower energy orbitals completely before moving to higher energy orbitals.
 - Sequence (using $n+l$ rule): $1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d \dots$
2. **Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity:** If degenerate orbitals (orbitals with the exact same energy, like $p_x, p_y, p_z$) are available, electrons will occupy them singly first, with parallel spins, before they begin to pair up.
3. **Pauli's Exclusion Principle:** No two electrons in the same atom can have the exact same set of all four quantum numbers. Consequently, an orbital can hold a maximum of 2 electrons, and they must have opposite spins.

### 2.7.2 Determining Electronic Configuration
*Always use the $(n+l)$ rule to determine energy levels.*
- **Carbon (Z=6):** $1s^2, 2s^2, 2p^2$ (Hund's rule dictates electrons in $p$ are $p_x^1, p_y^1, p_z^0$).
- **Oxygen (Z=8):** $1s^2, 2s^2, 2p^4$.

** High-Yield Exceptions:**
Half-filled and fully-filled $d$-subshells are exceptionally stable.
- **Chromium (Z=24):** Expected $4s^2 3d^4$ $\rightarrow$ **Actual: $4s^1 3d^5$** (Half-filled stability).
- **Copper (Z=29):** Expected $4s^2 3d^9$ $\rightarrow$ **Actual: $4s^1 3d^{10}$** (Fully-filled stability).
