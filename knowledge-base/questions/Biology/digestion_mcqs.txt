The digestive system consists of the GI tract and what other group of organs?

Visceral support organs

Major endocrine organs

Abdominal cavity organs

**Accessory digestive organs**

**Explanation:**

The digestive system is divided into the GI tract (a continuous tube) and accessory organs like the liver and pancreas that aid the process.

What is the correct anatomical sequence of the small intestine's segments?

Ileum, Jejunum, Duodenum

Jejunum, Ileum, Duodenum

Duodenum, Ileum, Jejunum

**Duodenum, Jejunum, Ileum**

**Explanation:**

The correct order is Duodenum (first), Jejunum (middle), and Ileum (last).

The vermiform appendix is a small, blind tube attached to which structure?

Sigmoid colon

Stomach pylorus

**Explanation:**

The vermiform appendix is attached to the cecum, which is the first part of the large intestine.

Which opening marks the junction between the esophagus and the stomach?

Pyloric opening

Ileocecal junction

Oral fauces

**Gastro-oesophageal opening**

**Explanation:**

This opening, also called the cardiac opening, is where the esophagus joins the stomach.

From the lumen outward, what is the correct order of the GI tract's four layers?

Serosa, Muscularis, Submucosa, Mucosa

Submucosa, Muscularis, Mucosa, Serosa

Muscularis, Mucosa, Serosa, Submucosa

**Mucosa, Submucosa, Muscularis, Serosa**

**Explanation:**

The correct sequence from the inside out is Mucosa, Submucosa, Muscularis, and Serosa.

What is the primary function of the stratified squamous epithelium lining the mouth?

Secretion of enzymes

Absorption of nutrients

Detection of taste

**Protection from abrasion**

**Explanation:**

The multiple layers of this epithelium are ideal for resisting the physical wear and tear from chewing.

What is the fleshy projection that hangs from the soft palate?

Palatine tonsil

**Explanation:**

The uvula is the projection from the soft palate that helps block the nasal cavity during swallowing.

The common hepatic duct and the cystic duct merge to form which duct?

Pancreatic duct

Hepatic portal vein

Right hepatic duct

**Common bile duct**

**Explanation:**

The common bile duct is formed by the union of the liver's common hepatic duct and the gallbladder's cystic duct.

What is the main digestive function of the liver?

Insulin secretion

Enzyme production

Chyme storage

**Bile production**

**Explanation:**

The liver's primary role in digestion is to produce bile, which is essential for fat emulsification.

Salivary amylase begins the chemical digestion of which macromolecule?

Nucleic acids

**Explanation:**

Salivary amylase specifically targets starch (a carbohydrate) and breaks it down into smaller sugars.

What is the main purpose of mastication (chewing)?

Begin protein digestion

Mix food with stomach acid

Absorb food nutrients

**Increase food surface area**

**Explanation:**

Chewing breaks large food particles into smaller ones, providing more surface area for digestive enzymes to act upon.

What is the term for the chewed, moistened ball of food that is swallowed?

**Bolus**

**Explanation:**

A bolus is the mass of chewed food that is pushed to the back of the throat for swallowing.

Which structure elevates to block the nasal cavity during swallowing?

Epiglottis

Larynx

Tongue

**Soft palate**

**Explanation:**

Muscles raise the soft palate to close off the nasopharynx, preventing food from entering the nasal passages.

Which flap of cartilage covers the airway during swallowing?

Uvula

Soft palate

Pharynx

**Epiglottis**

**Explanation:**

The epiglottis folds down over the larynx to ensure food travels down the esophagus and not the trachea.

What are the rhythmic, wave-like muscular contractions that move food through the GI tract?

Segmentation

Mastication

Deglutition

**Peristalsis**

**Explanation:**

Peristalsis is the coordinated muscular wave that propels food forward through the digestive tube.

Which stomach cells are responsible for secreting hydrochloric acid (HCl)?

Chief cells

Goblet cells

Endocrine cells

**Parietal cells**

**Explanation:**

Parietal (or oxyntic) cells are specialized epithelial cells in the stomach that secrete HCl and intrinsic factor.

Chief (zymogenic) cells in the stomach secrete which inactive enzyme?

Trypsinogen

Gastrin

Mucus

**Pepsinogen**

**Explanation:**

Chief cells secrete pepsinogen, which is the inactive precursor to the protein-digesting enzyme pepsin.

What is a primary function of hydrochloric acid in the stomach?

Begin carbohydrate digestion

Emulsify lipids

Absorb vitamin B12

**Kill bacteria and activate pepsin**

**Explanation:**

HCl creates a low pH environment that kills most ingested bacteria and converts inactive pepsinogen into active pepsin.

What is the semi-fluid, acidic mixture of food and gastric secretions in the stomach called?

Bolus

Bile

Faeces

**Chyme**

**Explanation:**

Ingested food and stomach secretions mix to form a semi-fluid material called chyme.

Which substance is essential for the absorption of vitamin B12 in the ileum?

Gastrin

Mucin

Secretin

**Intrinsic factor**

**Explanation:**

Intrinsic factor, secreted by parietal cells, binds with vitamin B12 to allow its absorption.

How is the stomach's epithelial wall protected from its own acid?

By a keratinized outer layer

By rapid water secretion

By the presence of bile salts

**By a thick mucus layer**

**Explanation:**

A thick layer of viscous and alkaline mucus protects the stomach wall from the damaging effect of acid and pepsin.

What is the optimal pH for the enzymatic activity of pepsin?

Around 4.5

Around 6.5

7.0 or more

**3.0 or less**

**Explanation:**

Pepsin functions effectively in the highly acidic conditions of the stomach, with peak activity at a very low pH.

The low pH of the stomach inactivates which enzyme from the oral cavity?

Pepsin

Lipase

Trypsin

**Salivary amylase**

**Explanation:**

Salivary amylase, which works at a neutral pH, is denatured by the strong acidity of the stomach.

Where does the greatest amount of nutrient absorption occur?

Stomach

Large intestine

Esophagus

**Small intestine**

**Explanation:**

The small intestine, with its massive surface area, is the site where most digestion and absorption takes place.

Which two segments of the small intestine are the major sites of nutrient absorption?

Jejunum and Ileum

Ileum and Cecum

Duodenum and Ileum

**Duodenum and Jejunum**

**Explanation:**

The text explicitly states that the duodenum and jejunum are the primary locations for absorbing digested nutrients.

The presence of acidic chyme in the duodenum stimulates the release of which hormone?

Gastrin

Insulin

Cholecystokinin (CCK)

**Secretin**

**Explanation:**

Secretin is released in response to acid and signals the pancreas to secrete bicarbonate to neutralize the chyme.

The arrival of fats in the duodenum initiates the release of which two hormones?

Secretin and Gastrin

Insulin and Glucagon

Somatostatin and Pepsin

**GIP and Cholecystokinin**

**Explanation:**

Fats and lipids trigger the release of both gastric inhibitory peptide (GIP) and cholecystokinin (CCK).

What is the primary role of bile salts in digestion?

Digest proteins

Neutralize chyme

Break down starches

**Emulsify fats**

**Explanation:**

Bile salts break down large fat masses into smaller droplets, a process called emulsification, increasing the surface area for enzymes.

What is the main function of the gallbladder?

Produce bile

Produce digestive enzymes

Detoxify chemicals

**Store and concentrate bile**

**Explanation:**

The gallbladder stores bile from the liver and concentrates it by absorbing water and electrolytes.

The bile pigment bilirubin is formed from the breakdown of what molecule?

Glycogen

Albumin

Cholesterol

**Hemoglobin**

**Explanation:**

When old red blood cells are broken down, the heme portion of hemoglobin is converted into bilirubin.

What is the process of converting excess glucose into glycogen in the liver called?

Glycogenolysis

Gluconeogenesis

Glycolysis

**Glycogenesis**

**Explanation:**

Glycogenesis (glycogen formation) is the synthesis of glycogen from glucose molecules for storage.

Which organ synthesizes blood clotting proteins like prothrombin and fibrinogen?

Pancreas

Bone marrow

Spleen

**Liver**

**Explanation:**

The liver synthesizes most plasma proteins, including the essential factors for the blood coagulation cascade.

In the liver, toxic ammonia is converted into which less toxic substance?

Uric acid

Creatinine

Bilirubin

**Urea**

**Explanation:**

The liver's urea cycle combines ammonia with CO2 to form urea, which is safely transported in the blood.

What is the main function of bicarbonate ions in pancreatic juice?

Emulsify fats

Digest proteins

Activate enzymes

**Neutralize acidic chyme**

**Explanation:**

Bicarbonate raises the pH of acidic chyme from the stomach, creating an optimal environment for pancreatic enzymes.

The inactive enzyme trypsinogen is activated to trypsin by what?

Pepsin

Chymotrypsin

Lipase

**Enterokinase**

**Explanation:**

Enterokinase, an enzyme on the brush border of the small intestine, activates trypsinogen to start the protease cascade.

Which pancreatic enzyme continues the digestion of polysaccharides?

Pancreatic lipase

Trypsin

Nuclease

**Pancreatic amylase**

**Explanation:**

Pancreatic amylase continues to break down starches and glycogen into smaller sugars in the small intestine.

Why are proteases like trypsin secreted from the pancreas in an inactive form?

To conserve metabolic energy

To function in an acidic pH

To allow for longer storage

**To prevent pancreatic self-digestion**

**Explanation:**

If secreted in their active forms, these enzymes would digest the proteins of the pancreatic cells, causing autodigestion.

What are the final absorbable products of carbohydrate digestion?

Polysaccharides

Disaccharides

Amino acids

**Monosaccharides**

**Explanation:**

Polysaccharides must be broken down into their simplest units, monosaccharides (like glucose), to be absorbed.

What are the tiny structures formed by bile salts that ferry lipids to intestinal cells?

Chylomicrons

Lipoproteins

Lacteals

**Micelles**

**Explanation:**

Micelles are microscopic aggregates that solubilize lipids and facilitate their contact with the absorptive cells.

Inside intestinal cells, absorbed fats are packaged with proteins into what structures?

Micelles

Free fatty acids

HDLs

**Chylomicrons**

**Explanation:**

Chylomicrons are lipoprotein particles assembled within intestinal cells to transport dietary lipids into the lymphatic system.

Unlike other nutrients, absorbed fats in chylomicrons first enter the:

Hepatic portal vein

Blood capillaries

Gallbladder

**Lymphatic system**

**Explanation:**

Due to their large size, chylomicrons are transported via lymphatic vessels (lacteals), bypassing the liver initially.

Where does the final breakdown of dipeptides and tripeptides into amino acids occur?

In the intestinal lumen

In the stomach

In the liver

**Inside intestinal epithelial cells**

**Explanation:**

Dipeptides and tripeptides are absorbed into intestinal cells, where intracellular enzymes complete their digestion.

By which process is water primarily absorbed from the digestive tract?

Active transport

Facilitated diffusion

Endocytosis

**Osmosis**

**Explanation:**

Water follows the movement of absorbed solutes, moving down its concentration gradient via osmosis.

What are the primary functions of the large intestine?

Protein digestion

Nutrient absorption

Enzyme secretion

**Water absorption and feces formation**

**Explanation:**

The large intestine absorbs water and electrolytes from chyme to form solid feces for elimination.

What stimulus directly initiates the defecation reflex?

Contraction of the stomach

Hormones from the duodenum

A conscious decision

**Stretching of the rectal wall**

**Explanation:**

The distention of the rectum's wall by feces stimulates stretch receptors, triggering the reflex.

Which anal sphincter is under voluntary, conscious control?

Internal anal sphincter

Pyloric sphincter

Ileocecal sphincter

**External anal sphincter**

**Explanation:**

The external anal sphincter is made of skeletal muscle, which allows for conscious control over defecation.

Bacteria in the colon synthesize which essential, fat-soluble vitamin?

Vitamin C

Vitamin D

Vitamin B12

**Vitamin K**

**Explanation:**

Some bacteria in the intestine synthesize vitamin K, which is then passively absorbed in the colon.

Which stomach hormone stimulates secretion from both parietal and chief cells?

Secretin

Cholecystokinin

Insulin

**Gastrin**

**Explanation:**

Gastrin acts on the stomach to powerfully stimulate the secretion of both hydrochloric acid and pepsinogen.

What are the primary stimuli for the greatest volume of gastric secretion?

The sight and smell of food

Chyme entering the duodenum

Stress and other emotions

**Stomach distention and amino acids**

**Explanation:**

The presence of food, causing distention and introducing peptides, is the main trigger for the gastric phase of secretion.

Cholecystokinin (CCK) stimulates the pancreas to release a secretion rich in what?

Bicarbonate ions

Water

Mucus

**Digestive enzymes**

**Explanation:**

CCK is the main stimulus for the acinar cells of the pancreas to secrete their enzymatic component.

The bacterium \*Helicobacter pylori\* is the most important factor in which disorder?

Food poisoning

Dyspepsia

Appendicitis

**Peptic ulcer disease**

**Explanation:**

The text states that H. pylori accounts for 90% of duodenal ulcers and 70% of gastric ulcers.

Which eating disorder involves binging followed by self-induced vomiting or purging?

Anorexia nervosa

Obesity

Dyspepsia

**Bulimia nervosa**

**Explanation:**

Bulimia nervosa is characterized by bouts of overeating followed by compensatory behaviors like purging.

What is the medical term for incomplete or imperfect digestion causing abdominal discomfort?

Ulcer

Food poisoning

Peritonitis

**Dyspepsia**

**Explanation:**

Dyspepsia is the general term for indigestion and its associated symptoms.

Blockage of the common bile duct would most severely impair the digestion of what?

Proteins

Carbohydrates

Nucleic acids

**Lipids**

**Explanation:**

Bile is essential for emulsifying fats. Without it, fat digestion by lipase would be extremely inefficient.

Which organ produces both digestive enzymes and acid-neutralizing bicarbonate?

Liver

Stomach

Small intestine

**Pancreas**

**Explanation:**

The pancreas secretes both an enzymatic component and an aqueous bicarbonate component into the duodenum.

A hiatal hernia involves a portion of the stomach protruding through the:

Pyloric sphincter

Ileocecal valve

Abdominal wall

**Oesophageal hiatus**

**Explanation:**

This condition involves the widening of the opening in the diaphragm for the esophagus.

What is the primary function of the ileocecal sphincter?

Control bile release

Control stomach emptying

Control chyme flow to the illeum

**Control chyme flow to the cecum**

**Explanation:**

This sphincter regulates the passage of material from the ileum to the large intestine.

A chylomicron, which is mostly lipid, would be classified as having what type of density?

High density (HDL)

Low density (LDL)

Intermediate density

**Extremely very low density**

**Explanation:**

Because lipids are less dense than proteins, a particle made almost entirely of lipid has a very low density.

Which hormone primarily stimulates the pancreas to release a bicarbonate-rich secretion?

Gastrin

Cholecystokinin

Gastric inhibitory peptide

**Secretin**

**Explanation:**

Secretin is the principal hormone that stimulates pancreatic duct cells to secrete bicarbonate.

An inability to produce intrinsic factor would lead to a deficiency in which vitamin?

Vitamin K

Vitamin C

Vitamin A

**Vitamin B12**

**Explanation:**

Intrinsic factor is absolutely required for the absorption of Vitamin B12 in the ileum.

The liver can synthesize Vitamin A from which precursor molecule?

Amino acids

Fatty acids

Cholesterol

**Carotene**

**Explanation:**

The text states that the synthesis of Vitamin A from carotene takes place in the liver.

The reversal of peristalsis, known as antiperistalsis, can result in what action?

Diarrhea

Absorption

Constipation

**Vomiting**

**Explanation:**

Antiperistalsis is the physiological mechanism that forcefully moves stomach contents upward, leading to vomiting.

Where does the final digestion of proteins into single amino acids occur?

In the pancreatic juice

In the gastric juice

Within the liver cells

**At the intestinal brush border**

**Explanation:**

Peptidases bound to the microvilli (brush border) complete the breakdown of small peptides.

Why is defecation an automatic reflex in infants?

Lack of control over the internal anal sphincter

Lack of control over the ileocecal sphincter

Lack of control over the rectal muscles

**Lack of control over the external anal sphincter**

**Explanation:**

Voluntary control of the skeletal muscle of the external anal sphincter is not yet developed in infants.

Which substance synthesized by the liver acts as an anticoagulant?

Fibrinogen

Prothrombin

Vitamin K

**Heparin**

**Explanation:**

Heparin, produced by the liver, prevents blood from clotting inside blood vessels.

The Nobel Prize in 2005 recognized the discovery of which bacterium's role in ulcers?

Salmonella enterica

Campylobacter jejuni

Escherichia coli

**Helicobacter pylori**

**Explanation:**

Barry Marshall and Robin Warren won the Nobel Prize for demonstrating that \*H. pylori\* is responsible for most peptic ulcers.

What are the three major pairs of salivary glands?

Buccal, lingual, and palatine

Gastric, duodenal, and intestinal

Hepatic, cystic, and pancreatic

**Parotid, submandibular, and sublingual**

**Explanation:**

The parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands are the three major pairs that produce the bulk of saliva.

What is the primary function of the gastric folds known as rugae?

Increase absorptive area

Secrete hydrochloric acid

Anchor the stomach wall

**Allow stomach expansion**

**Explanation:**

The rugae are folds that flatten out as the stomach fills, allowing it to increase its volume to accommodate a meal.

What type of epithelium covers the surface of an intestinal villus?

Stratified squamous

Simple cuboidal

Transitional

**Simple columnar**

**Explanation:**

The villus is covered by a single layer of simple columnar epithelial cells, specialized for absorption.

The endocrine function of the pancreas is performed by which structures?

Acini cells

Kupffer cells

Ductal cells

**Pancreatic islets**

**Explanation:**

The pancreatic islets (of Langerhans) are the distinct clusters of endocrine cells within the pancreas.

The stomach's muscularis layer contains a third layer of what type of muscle?

Skeletal muscle

Diagonal muscle

Transverse muscle

**Oblique muscle**

**Explanation:**

The stomach's three smooth muscle layers (longitudinal, circular, and oblique) allow for complex churning movements.

Besides stimulating the pancreas, cholecystokinin (CCK) also causes what action?

Stomach acid secretion

Hepatic glucose production

LES sphincter relaxation

**Gallbladder contraction**

**Explanation:**

CCK is the main hormone for gallbladder contraction, ensuring bile is released when fat is in the duodenum.

Immunoglobulin and lysozyme in saliva contribute to what function?

Digesting starch

Lubricating food

Neutralizing acids

**Preventing bacterial infection**

**Explanation:**

Immunoglobulins (antibodies) and lysozyme (an antibacterial enzyme) provide a first line of defense in the mouth.

How is ingested cellulose handled by the human digestive system?

It is digested by pancreatic amylase

It is absorbed in the large intestine

It is broken down by stomach acid

**It passes through largely undigested**

**Explanation:**

Humans lack the enzyme cellulase, so cellulose serves as dietary fiber and passes through mostly undigested.

The 'brush border' of the small intestine is formed by what structures?

Villi

Goblet cells

Lacteals

**Microvilli**

**Explanation:**

Microvilli are microscopic projections on absorptive cells that form the brush border, increasing surface area.

Anorexia nervosa is a serious eating disorder primarily characterized by what?

Binging and purging

Excessive food intake

Imperfect digestion

**Fear of becoming obese**

**Explanation:**

The core feature of anorexia nervosa is a distorted body image and an intense fear of gaining weight, leading to severe food restriction.

The removal of amino groups from amino acids, or deamination, occurs in which organ?

Kidneys

Small intestine

Stomach

**Liver**

**Explanation:**

The liver is the primary site for deamination, processing amino acids for energy use.

Which pancreatic cells produce the exocrine secretion known as pancreatic juice?

Islets of Langerhans

Parietal cells

Hepatocytes

**Acini cells**

**Explanation:**

The acinar cells are the exocrine cells of the pancreas that synthesize and secrete digestive enzymes.

Gluconeogenesis in the liver refers to the synthesis of glucose from what?

The breakdown of glycogen

Fructose and galactose

The breakdown of starch

**Non-carbohydrate sources**

**Explanation:**

Gluconeogenesis ('new glucose formation') creates glucose from sources like amino acids and glycerol.

What is the primary digestive function of the pharynx?

Secrete mucus and amylase

Begin protein digestion

Absorb water and electrolytes

**Act as a passageway for the bolus**

**Explanation:**

The pharynx is a muscular tube that propels the bolus from the oral cavity to the esophagus during swallowing.

What is the straight, muscular tube that precedes the anal canal?

Sigmoid colon

Appendix

Descending colon

**Rectum**

**Explanation:**

The rectum is the terminal section of the colon that stores feces before defecation.

What are the main products of lipid digestion by pancreatic lipase?

Amino acids

Monosaccharides

Bile salts and cholesterol

**Free fatty acids and glycerol**

**Explanation:**

Pancreatic lipase breaks down triacylglycerols into their main components: free fatty acids and glycerol.

What are the gentle, 20-second interval contractions in the stomach called?

Segmentation contractions

Mass movements

Tonic contractions

**Mixing waves**

**Explanation:**

These gentle waves mix ingested material with gastric secretions, distinct from stronger peristaltic waves.

Which group of vitamins is stored in the liver?

C and most B vitamins

A and C only

K and B6 only

**A, D, E, K, and B12**

**Explanation:**

The text lists the fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and vitamin B12 as being stored by hepatocytes.

Food poisoning from \*Salmonella\* often occurs due to what?

Excessive stomach acid

An autoimmune reaction

Self-induced vomiting

**Cross-contamination from raw meat**

**Explanation:**

The text highlights the risk of liquid from defrosting meat containing bacteria contaminating other foods or utensils.

What is the medical term for having an abnormal amount of body fat?

Anorexia nervosa

Dyspepsia

**Obesity**

**Explanation:**

Obesity is the term for having excess body fat and is linked to disorders like diabetes and cardiovascular disease.

Where are the phagocytic Kupffer cells that destroy old red blood cells located?

Spleen

Pancreas

Small intestine

**Liver**

**Explanation:**

Kupffer cells are the resident macrophages of the liver sinusoids.

What is the main role of the lower esophageal sphincter?

Regulate stomach emptying

Initiate swallowing

Mix the bolus with mucus

**Prevent acid reflux**

**Explanation:**

This sphincter remains contracted to prevent acidic stomach contents from backing up into the esophagus.

What is the term for gases produced by bacterial action in the colon?

Faeces

Chyme

**Explanation:**

The text defines flatus as the gases produced by microorganisms in the colon.

Which enzymes in pancreatic juice digest DNA and RNA?

Amylase and lipase

Trypsin and chymotrypsin

Pepsin and gastrin

**Deoxyribonucleases and ribonucleases**

**Explanation:**

These enzymes specifically break down DNA and RNA, respectively, into their component nucleotides.

Hyperplastic obesity is a condition characterized by what?

An increase in fat cell size

Uneven fat distribution

A decreased metabolic rate

**A greater number of fat cells**

**Explanation:**

Hyperplastic obesity is defined as having a greater than normal number of fat cells.
